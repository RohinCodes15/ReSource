# Problem statement

Every figure below is from a City of Fremont document or the news report named, as recorded in `shared/data/budget.json` with page numbers. Addresses are in `docs/DATA_SOURCES.md`. All were read on October 12, 2026.

## The problem

Fremont, California is a city of 232,619 people across 92 square miles [1]. Its FY 2026/27 proposed budget is $427.1 million, of which $289.1 million is the General Fund that pays for most day-to-day services [2].

That budget has been balanced by cutting:

- **$3.8 million** in operational reductions across every city department in FY 2025/26, covering staffing, overtime, training and contract services [1][3].
- **About $3 million** removed from pavement maintenance and park and street-median upkeep in FY 2025/26 [1].
- **Nine Police Department positions that are not sworn officers** eliminated in the February 10, 2026 rebalancing: six administrative positions, two detention officers and one detention supervisor [3].
- **Ten more Human Services positions** that the City Manager wrote would have to be eliminated because of State and federal funding changes [1].

The City's own documents explain why relief is not close. A 2022 State tax-agency error overpaid Fremont about $20 million, which the City is paying back from reserves [1]. In FY 2024/25 it applied $19.9 million from its Budget Uncertainty Reserve to cover a one-time sales tax shortfall, and that reserve was projected to end FY 2025/26 at $4.3 million [4]. The FY 2026/27 Proposed Budget says there is no capacity to restore eliminated positions or services [2]. In July 2026, news reports said the Council voted to defer a half-cent sales tax measure estimated to raise $30 million to $35 million a year [5].

## Why residents cannot easily act on it

All of that is public. None of it is easy to use. The facts are spread across a budget presentation, a mid-year report, a news release, a letter to state legislators and a community message, most of them PDFs written for people who already follow city finance. A resident who wants to know whether the change touches their street, their parent's senior center or their child's counseling program has to find and read all of them, in English.

Three gaps follow:

1. **No personal view.** The documents describe the budget by fund and department, not by what a household uses.
2. **No next step.** They say what was reduced, not where a resident can turn instead: a food bank, a clinic, a referral line.
3. **No way back.** There is no simple, structured way for residents to tell the City how a specific change affects them.

Fremont is also a city of many languages. This statement makes no claim about how many residents speak each one, because that figure is not in the project's sources; the app is offered in English, Spanish, Dari, Chinese and Vietnamese because those are the languages the project chose to support first.

## What ReSource does about it

- **What changed for me?** A resident picks an area and interests and sees the matching service changes, each with its source. Where the City has not published an amount or a date, the app says so.
- **Where to get help.** Fourteen organizations and phone lines, each taken from the organization's own page, sorted by distance.
- **Tell the City.** A structured report (service, area, impact level, description) with personal details removed, counted publicly only after a moderator verifies it, and a letter draft addressed to the resident's council member.

## What this statement does not claim

- It does not claim any number of residents affected by a given cut. The City has not published one.
- It does not claim any number of people using ReSource. It has not launched.
- It does not say how any council member voted. The official minutes were not read.
